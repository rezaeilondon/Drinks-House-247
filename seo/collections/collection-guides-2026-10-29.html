How to Choose a Vintage Wine

A vintage on the label means every grape in the bottle was picked in that one year. Weather shapes each harvest: a warm, dry summer gives riper, fuller wines, while a cool or wet year gives lighter wines that are often more fragrant. That is why two bottles from the same estate can taste quite different a few years apart, and why collectors follow particular years.

Ready to drink, or one to keep?

Most of our vintage red Bordeaux, Burgundy and Rhône are already in their drinking window. A wine that is ten to twenty years old has softer tannins and developed notes of leather, earth and dried fruit. Younger vintages from great years, such as 2015, 2016, 2019 and 2020 in Bordeaux, will keep improving in a cool, dark cellar for a decade or more.

Serving older vintages

Stand the bottle upright for a few hours so any sediment settles, then decant it slowly, leaving the last centimetre in the bottle. Serve reds at 16–18°C and whites at 10–12°C. Very old wines open up quickly, so pour soon after decanting.

Questions

Is an older vintage always better? No. Many wines are made to be enjoyed young. Age improves wines built for it, such as classed-growth Bordeaux, top Burgundy and vintage Champagne, and the quality of the year matters as much as the age.

A Guide to French Wine Regions

France makes more of the world's benchmark wines than any other country. Each region has its own grapes and style, so knowing the region tells you most of what is in the glass.

Choosing a 75cl Bottle of Champagne

The 75cl bottle is the standard Champagne size. It pours six flutes, or about eight smaller glasses for a toast. Everything in this collection, from house non-vintage to prestige cuvées, comes in this size.

Non-vintage or vintage?

Most Champagne is non-vintage: a blend of several harvests, made to taste the same every year in the house style. Moët & Chandon Impérial and Veuve Clicquot Yellow Label are examples. Vintage Champagne comes from a single, declared year and is aged longer, so it has more depth. Dom Pérignon only makes vintage wines.

How sweet will it taste?

Brut is dry and the most popular style. Extra Brut and Brut Nature are drier still. Demi-Sec is noticeably sweet and good with dessert. Rosé Champagne adds red-fruit flavour and a little more body; see our rosé Champagne.

Serving

Serve at 8–10°C: about three hours in the fridge, or 20–30 minutes in an ice bucket with water and ice. A tulip or white wine glass shows the aromas better than a narrow flute. Open it by holding the cork and turning the bottle, not the cork.

How many bottles do I need?

Plan on one bottle for every six glasses you want to pour. For a reception, allow about two glasses per guest in the first hour. For bigger groups, a magnum holds two bottles' worth and looks the part.

What Non-Vintage Means

A non-vintage wine is blended from more than one harvest. The winemaker keeps reserve wines from earlier years and mixes them with the new crop, so the wine tastes the same every time you buy it, whatever the weather did that year. Understanding Chablis

Chablis is the northernmost part of Burgundy, and every Chablis is 100% Chardonnay. The cool climate and the Kimmeridgian soil, a limestone and clay packed with tiny fossilised oyster shells, give the wines their crisp acidity and the flinty, saline quality often called minerality. Most producers use little or no new oak, so the fruit stays pure.

The four levels

  • Petit Chablis: light and fresh, for drinking young.
  • Chablis: the classic style, with green apple, lemon and a stony finish.
  • Chablis Premier Cru: named vineyards such as Montée de Tonnerre, Fourchaume, Vaillons and Mont de Milieu. More depth, and good for five to ten years.
  • Chablis Grand Cru: seven vineyards on one south-west-facing slope (Blanchot, Bougros, Les Clos, Grenouilles, Les Preuses, Valmur and Vaudésir). The most concentrated and longest-lived.

Food and serving

Chablis and oysters are a classic pairing. It also suits sushi, grilled white fish, goat's cheese and creamy chicken dishes. Serve Chablis and Petit Chablis at 8–10°C; give Premier and Grand Cru a little longer out of the fridge, at 11–13°C, so the flavours open. See also wines that pair with oysters.

A Guide to the Médoc

The Médoc is the gravel peninsula on the Left Bank of the Gironde, north of the city of Bordeaux. Its free-draining soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon, which leads most blends here, softened with Merlot and sometimes lifted with Cabernet Franc and Petit Verdot. The wines are structured and age-worthy, with blackcurrant, cedar and graphite notes.

The great communes

  • Saint-Estèphe: firm, robust and long-lived.
  • Pauillac: powerful classic Cabernet, home to three of the five First Growths.
  • Saint-Julien: balanced and polished, between power and elegance.
  • Margaux: the most perfumed and silky of the four.

Wines labelled Médoc or Haut-Médoc come from the wider area and often offer excellent value, while Listrac and Moulis sit inland.

The 1855 classification

In 1855 the leading Médoc châteaux were ranked into five growths, from Premier Cru (First Growth) to Cinquième Cru. The list has barely changed since. Many Cru Bourgeois estates, outside that ranking, make outstanding wine too.

Drinking and serving

Young Médoc benefits from decanting for an hour or more. From ten years old the tannins soften and savoury notes appear. Serve at 16–18°C with roast lamb, steak or hard cheeses.

Why Choose a Magnum of Champagne

A magnum holds 1.5 litres, the same as two standard bottles, and pours about twelve flutes. It brings a celebration together around one bottle, and it looks the part on a table or in a photo.

Magnums taste better

There is a reason many Champagne makers consider the magnum the ideal size. The neck is the same width as a standard bottle, so the wine inside has proportionally less contact with the air trapped under the cork. It ages more slowly and evenly, and keeps its freshness and fine bubbles for longer. Vintage Champagne in magnum is especially prized by collectors.

What Makes a Wine Vegetarian?

Wine is made from grapes, so it might seem vegetarian by default. The difference lies in fining: an optional step that clears haze from young wine before bottling. Some traditional fining agents come from animals.

Fining agents explained

  • Not vegetarian: gelatine (from animal bones and skin) and isinglass (from fish bladders).
  • Vegetarian but not vegan: egg whites, traditionally used for fine red Bordeaux and Burgundy, and casein from milk.
  • Vegan: bentonite clay, pea protein, activated charcoal, or no fining at all.

Many top estates in Burgundy, Chablis and Bordeaux now fine lightly, or not at all, and let the wine settle naturally over time. That is why so many of the region's great names appear in this collection.

A Guide to Vegan Wine

A vegan wine uses no animal products at any stage. The main thing to avoid is animal-based fining, a step that clears haze from young wine. Traditional agents include egg whites, milk protein (casein), gelatine and isinglass, from fish. Vegan winemakers use clay (bentonite), pea protein or charcoal instead, or skip fining altogether and let time do the work.

Does vegan wine taste different?

No. Fining agents are removed before bottling, so the choice has no effect on flavour. Unfined wines can show a little more texture or a light sediment, which many wine lovers see as a sign of minimal intervention.

A Guide to Japanese Sake

This collection currently features premium Japanese sake. Sake is brewed from polished rice, water, yeast and koji (a mould that turns rice starch into sugar). It is usually 14–16% alcohol, similar to a strong wine.

Reading a sake label

The grade depends on how much of each rice grain is polished away. The more is removed, the more delicate and fragrant the sake.

  • Junmai: pure rice sake with no added distilled alcohol. Full and savoury.
  • Ginjo / Junmai Ginjo: rice polished to 60% or less of its original size. Lighter and fruity, with notes of melon, pear and apple.
  • Daiginjo / Junmai Daiginjo: polished to 50% or less. The most refined style, silky and aromatic.

How to serve it

Premium Ginjo and Daiginjo sake is best lightly chilled, at around 8–12°C, in a wine glass or small cup. Warming suits fuller Junmai styles, but it hides the delicate aromas of top grades. Keep sake in the fridge and drink it within a few weeks of opening.

Food pairing

Sake goes beautifully with sushi, sashimi and tempura, and with oysters, soft cheeses and roast chicken. Its gentle umami also flatters dishes that are hard to match with wine.

A Guide to Pomerol

A Guide to Saint-Émilion

Understanding the labels

A Guide to Marlborough

Marlborough sits at the top of New Zealand's South Island and is the country's largest wine region. Sauvignon Blanc was first planted here in the 1970s, and within a few decades Marlborough had created a style copied around the world.

Why it tastes the way it does

A Guide to Vermouth and Aperitifs

A Guide to Port, Madeira and Portuguese Wine

Portugal is home to two of the world's great fortified wines. Port comes from the steep terraces of the Douro Valley; Madeira from the Atlantic island of the same name.

Port styles

Port is made by adding grape spirit during fermentation, which stops it early and leaves natural sweetness. Taylor's, founded in 1692, is one of the oldest Port houses.

  • Reserve and aged tawny: aged for years in wooden casks, turning amber and nutty, with caramel and dried fruit. A 20 Year Old tawny is rich and silky.
  • Late Bottled Vintage (LBV): from a single year, aged four to six years in wood, ready to drink and fruity.
  • Vintage Port: made only in the best years and bottled young to age for decades. 2007 is a widely acclaimed vintage.

Madeira

Madeira is aged with gentle warmth, which makes it almost indestructible: an opened bottle keeps for months. Malmsey is the richest, sweetest style, with toffee, orange peel and a tangy finish. Blandy's has made Madeira since 1811.

Serving

A Guide to Sangiovese

A Guide to Nebbiolo

A Guide to Riesling

Riesling is often called the greatest white grape in the world. It is rarely aged in oak, so it shows pure fruit and the character of its vineyard: lime, green apple, peach and white flowers, with racy acidity. A Guide to the Rhône Valley

The Rhône flows south from Lyon to the Mediterranean, and its vineyards fall into two very different halves.

Northern Rhône: Syrah

Steep granite slopes produce some of the world's finest Syrah: dark, peppery and perfumed, with violet, blackberry and smoked meat. The great names include Côte-Rôtie, Hermitage, Cornas and Crozes-Hermitage. White wines come from Viognier and Marsanne.

Southern Rhône: Grenache blends

The warmer south makes generous, spicy blends, led by Grenache with Syrah and Mourvèdre. Châteauneuf-du-Pape, named after the summer palace of the medieval popes, is the most famous. Up to 13 grape varieties are permitted, and many vineyards are covered with large round stones that store the day's heat. Côtes-du-Rhône offers the same warm, fruity style at an everyday price.

Guigal

E. Guigal, based in Ampuis in the northern Rhône, is the region's best-known producer. Its Côte-Rôtie wines are legendary, and its Côtes-du-Rhône Rouge is a benchmark for the appellation.

What Does "Practising Organic" Mean?

Many fine estates farm without synthetic pesticides, herbicides or fertilisers, but choose not to apply for formal certification, or are still in the three-year conversion period. Their wines are "practising organic": made with the same care for the soil, without the logo on the label.

Organic, biodynamic and sustainable

  • Organic: no synthetic chemicals in the vineyard, with natural treatments and composts instead. See certified organic wines.
  • Biodynamic: organic farming plus herbal preparations and a calendar based on natural cycles. Château Palmer in Margaux is one of the best-known Bordeaux estates to farm this way. See biodynamic wines.
  • Sustainable: a broader approach covering water, energy and biodiversity. See sustainable wines.

What you will find here

Does organic wine taste better?

Organic farming does not guarantee a better wine, but many growers find that healthy, living soils give more vibrant grapes and a clearer sense of place. It is also better for the vineyard's wildlife and workers.
